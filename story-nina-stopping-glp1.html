Stopping · Weight Regain

"I Stopped Ozempic After 14 Months. Here's What Happened Next."

Jan 8, 2026·10 min read
At a glance: Nina P., 39, lost 48 lbs on Ozempic over 14 months. She felt great, hit her goal weight, and decided she didn't need the medication anymore. Within six months of stopping, she regained 31 lbs. The food noise came back. The cravings came back. Everything she thought she'd "fixed" came back. Here's the honest, uncomfortable truth about what happens when you stop — and why she ultimately went back on a lower dose.

Why I thought I could stop

I'd been on Ozempic for 14 months. I'd lost 48 lbs. My relationship with food felt transformed. I was eating smaller portions naturally, making better choices without effort, and the constant mental chatter about food — what to eat, when to eat, whether I should eat — had gone quiet. I felt like a different person.

And that was the problem. I felt like a different person. So I assumed I was a different person. I thought the habits I'd built on Ozempic were mine now — that the medication had taught me how to eat and I'd graduated. Like training wheels on a bike.

I genuinely believed I'd rewired my brain. That the 14 months on Ozempic had permanently changed how I thought about food. I was wrong. What I'd actually done was confuse the medication's effect for personal transformation.

My doctor was cautious. She said the data on stopping GLP-1 medications was clear — most people regain a significant portion of the weight within a year. She suggested stepping down to a lower maintenance dose rather than stopping entirely. I heard her. I appreciated the concern. And I decided I'd be the exception.

The first two weeks: fine

The first couple of weeks off Ozempic were unremarkable. I felt normal. My appetite was maybe slightly increased, but nothing alarming. I thought: "See? I was right. The habits stuck."

Looking back, this was the semaglutide still in my system. The medication has a long half-life — it takes several weeks to fully clear. Those two "fine" weeks weren't evidence that I'd changed. They were the medication's afterglow.

Weeks 3–6: the return of food noise

It started gradually. A thought here, a craving there. Nothing dramatic at first — just a growing awareness that food was taking up more mental space than it had in over a year.

By week four, the food noise was back. Not building slowly anymore — it was just there, like someone had flipped a switch. I'd be in a meeting at work and thinking about what I was going to eat for lunch. I'd walk past a bakery and have to talk myself out of going in. I'd finish dinner and immediately think about what else I could eat.

This was the most disorienting part. On Ozempic, food noise had simply... stopped. I'd forgotten what it felt like. Now it was back, and it was louder than I remembered — or maybe I just noticed it more because I'd experienced what quiet sounded like.

What is "food noise"? Food noise is the persistent, intrusive mental chatter about food — thinking about what to eat, when to eat, wanting to eat even when you're not hungry. It's not about willpower or discipline. For many people, it's a neurological pattern driven by hormones like ghrelin and leptin, and by the brain's reward pathways. GLP-1 medications quiet this noise by acting on the brain's appetite-regulation centres. When the medication is removed, the noise typically returns because the underlying biology hasn't changed.

Months 2–4: the regain begins

I gained 8 lbs in the second month off Ozempic. I told myself it was water weight, hormonal fluctuation, normal variation. It wasn't.

By month three, I was eating portions that would have been unthinkable six months earlier. Not bingeing — I want to be clear about that. I wasn't eating out of control. I was eating what felt like normal portions. The problem was that "normal" without Ozempic was significantly more food than "normal" with it. My baseline had shifted back.

The cravings were the worst part. On Ozempic, I could walk past a bag of crisps in the cupboard and not think about it. Now I could hear them calling from two rooms away. Sugary foods, carb-heavy foods, the exact things that Ozempic had made feel irrelevant — they were relevant again. Powerfully relevant.

By month four, I'd regained 19 lbs. I was still lighter than my starting weight, but the trajectory was unmistakable. And the speed of it was frightening.

The emotional toll

The regain itself was difficult. But the shame was worse. I'd been so confident I'd be the exception. I'd told friends I was stopping. I'd told my mum. I'd told colleagues who'd commented on my weight loss. Now I was watching the weight come back, and every pound felt like a public admission that I'd failed.

The hardest part wasn't the weight coming back. It was the feeling that I'd proved something about myself that I didn't want to be true — that without the medication, I couldn't do it. That the "new me" had been pharmaceutical, not personal.

I avoided social situations where people might notice. I stopped wearing the clothes I'd bought at my lowest weight. I started making excuses not to see friends. The confidence I'd built over 14 months was eroding in weeks.

Month 6: the decision to go back

Six months after stopping, I'd regained 31 of the 48 lbs I'd lost. My food noise was as loud as it had ever been. My blood work was trending in the wrong direction — cholesterol up, fasting glucose creeping higher. The trajectory was clear, and it wasn't going to reverse itself through willpower.

I went back to my doctor. She was kind about it — no "I told you so," just a straightforward conversation about options. She suggested restarting at a lower maintenance dose of 0.5mg rather than going back to the full 1.0mg I'd been on. The idea was that a lower dose might be enough to maintain without the full intensity of treatment-level dosing.

What coming back on feels like

Restarting was both a relief and a defeat. The relief was immediate — within two weeks on 0.5mg, the food noise quieted again. Not as dramatically as the first time, but enough. The cravings softened. The constant mental negotiation about food faded into background noise. I could think about other things again.

The defeat took longer to process. I'd wanted so badly to be someone who could manage without medication. Going back felt like giving in. My therapist (the same one I'd been seeing for other reasons) reframed it in a way that helped: "You wouldn't stop taking blood pressure medication because your blood pressure improved. The improvement is the medication working."

She was right. But knowing that intellectually and accepting it emotionally are two different things.

The maintenance dose approach

I've been back on Ozempic for three months now, at 0.5mg instead of the 1.0mg I was on before. The experience is different this time:

  • Appetite suppression is milder. I'm not as aggressively appetite-suppressed as I was on 1.0mg. I eat more than I did at peak Ozempic, but substantially less than I was eating off it. It feels sustainable rather than extreme.
  • Weight loss is slower. I've lost about 13 lbs in three months back on — much slower than my initial round. My doctor says this is normal for restart and for a lower dose. The goal now is stabilisation, not rapid loss.
  • Side effects are minimal. Barely any nausea this time around. Whether that's the lower dose or my body remembering the medication, I don't know. But restarting was physically easy.
  • My mindset is different. I'm not planning to stop again. That's the biggest change. I've accepted that this is likely a long-term medication for me, not a temporary intervention. That acceptance has actually been freeing — I've stopped viewing it as something I need to escape from.

What I wish someone had told me

  • The habits aren't yours — they're the medication's. The portion control, the reduced cravings, the quiet food noise — those feel like personal achievements while you're on the medication. They're not. They're pharmacological effects. When the medication goes, they go with it. The exceptions to this are rare.
  • The "training wheels" metaphor is wrong. GLP-1 medications don't teach your brain new habits that persist after stopping. They change your brain chemistry while you're taking them. A better metaphor is glasses: they correct your vision while you're wearing them, but your eyes don't improve when you take them off.
  • Your doctor's caution isn't pessimism. When my doctor suggested a maintenance dose instead of stopping, she wasn't being negative. She was reading the data accurately. Clinical trials consistently show that two-thirds of weight lost on GLP-1 medications is regained within a year of stopping. I thought I'd be in the other third. I wasn't.
  • Regain is not failure. This was the hardest thing to accept. Regaining weight after stopping a GLP-1 isn't a character flaw or a lack of willpower. It's the predicted biological outcome of removing a medication that was treating an ongoing condition. Obesity is chronic. Treating it with a medication and then stopping is like treating hypertension with a pill and then wondering why your blood pressure went back up.
  • A maintenance dose is a legitimate strategy. You don't have to choose between full-dose treatment and nothing. Many doctors are now prescribing lower maintenance doses for patients who've reached their goals. This is emerging as a sensible long-term approach — enough medication to maintain the benefits without the intensity (or cost) of the full treatment dose.
  • Going back on isn't starting over. Restarting felt like failure at first. It wasn't. I still have the foundation from my first 14 months. The restart is faster and easier. Going back is not the same as going back to square one.
If you're thinking about stopping your GLP-1 medication: Talk to your doctor first. Ask about stepping down to a maintenance dose rather than stopping entirely. Have a realistic conversation about the likelihood of regain. If you do decide to stop, monitor your weight closely in the first few months — if it starts trending upward, that's the time to intervene, not after you've regained 30 lbs. Early action is everything.

Where I am now

I'm at 183 lbs — down from 196 when I restarted, still above my lowest of 165. I may never get back to 165, and I'm making peace with that. My doctor says a stable weight in the mid-170s on a maintenance dose would be an excellent outcome. I'm inclined to agree.

The biggest change isn't physical. It's philosophical. I've stopped thinking of Ozempic as a temporary fix and started thinking of it as ongoing treatment for a chronic condition. That shift in framing has removed the pressure to "graduate" from the medication, and with it, most of the shame.

I'm not the exception I wanted to be. But I'm managing my health, I'm maintaining most of the weight I lost, and I'm doing it without the constant guilt of feeling like I should be able to do this on my own. That's enough. It's more than enough.
