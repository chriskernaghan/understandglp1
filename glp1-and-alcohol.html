GLP-1 and Alcohol: What the Research Really Shows

March 2026·12 min read

People on Ozempic and Wegovy have been reporting it for years: the desire to drink just fades. A glass of wine feels like enough. A night that used to mean six drinks ends at two. Many noticed it before researchers did — but science is now catching up, and what it's finding is striking.

GLP-1 medications appear to meaningfully reduce alcohol cravings and consumption, not as a side effect exactly, but as a consequence of how these drugs interact with the brain's reward system. The evidence now spans randomised controlled trials, massive real-world registry studies, and a plausible biological mechanism. Here's a thorough look at where the science stands.

TL;DR: Multiple lines of evidence — from a 2025 JAMA Psychiatry trial to a Swedish registry study of 227,000 people — show GLP-1 medications significantly reduce alcohol cravings and consumption. The effect is real and increasingly well-documented, though large Phase 3 trials are still needed before these drugs can be formally approved for alcohol use disorder.

Why would a diabetes drug affect drinking?

GLP-1 receptors aren't only in your gut. They're distributed throughout the brain, including in the mesolimbic dopamine system — the reward circuit that makes pleasurable things feel rewarding. This is the same network involved in addiction to alcohol, nicotine, opioids, and other substances.

When you drink alcohol, dopamine is released in this system, creating the pleasurable sensation that reinforces the behaviour. GLP-1 receptor agonists appear to dampen that dopamine response — reducing how rewarding alcohol feels, and by extension, how much you want it.

This isn't a new hypothesis. Preclinical research in animal models showed GLP-1 receptor agonists reducing alcohol intake years before human trials began. What's changed recently is that the human data has started to arrive — and it supports the theory.

Why "food noise" and alcohol cravings may share the same mechanism: The same brain circuitry that drives compulsive eating drives compulsive drinking. GLP-1 medications appear to quiet both, which is why many patients report a broad dampening of compulsive urges — not just for food.

The clinical trial evidence

The JAMA Psychiatry trial (2025)

In February 2025, the first randomised controlled trial of semaglutide for alcohol use disorder was published in JAMA Psychiatry. Conducted at the University of North Carolina and led by researchers Christian Hendershot and Klara Klein, the trial enrolled 48 adults with alcohol use disorder and randomly assigned them to nine weeks of weekly semaglutide injections or placebo.

Crucially, the doses used were low — 0.25mg and 0.5mg per week, far below the 2.4mg dose used for weight loss in Wegovy. Despite this, the results were clear. Compared to placebo, participants on semaglutide consumed less alcohol during a controlled laboratory drinking session, reported fewer drinks on drinking days, and had significantly lower alcohol cravings over the course of treatment.

Perhaps most clinically meaningful: by the final month of treatment, around 40% of people in the semaglutide group reported no heavy drinking days at all. In the placebo group, that figure was 20%.

There were also signals beyond alcohol. In the subgroup who smoked, semaglutide led to greater reductions in cigarettes per day compared to placebo — an early hint that the effect may extend across addictive behaviours.

What the trial didn't show: Semaglutide didn't significantly increase the number of days completely abstinent from alcohol. It reduced how much people drank when they did drink, and reduced cravings — but it didn't make people stop drinking entirely. This is an important nuance when interpreting the results.

What the trial's limitations mean

The trial was small — 48 participants — and relatively short at nine weeks. The participants were also described as non-treatment-seeking, meaning they weren't people who had sought help for alcohol use disorder; they were recruited specifically for the study. Their baseline drinking levels were lower than those typically seen in treatment settings.

These limitations don't invalidate the findings, but they do mean we should hold them as promising evidence rather than definitive proof. The researchers were clear about this, describing the results as justifying larger trials rather than confirming a treatment. Those larger trials are now underway.

The real-world data

The Nature Communications study

While randomised trials are the gold standard for establishing causation, large real-world datasets can reveal effects at a scale that trials cannot. A 2024 study published in Nature Communications analysed electronic health records of over 83,000 patients with obesity and found that semaglutide users had a 50–56% lower risk of both developing and relapsing into alcohol use disorder over a 12-month follow-up period, compared to people taking other anti-obesity medications.

The finding was consistent across gender, age group, and racial subgroups, and held in people both with and without type 2 diabetes. A separate, even larger replication in nearly 600,000 patients with type 2 diabetes produced similar results.

The Swedish registry study

A Swedish nationwide registry study — following over 227,000 people with alcohol use disorder for a median of nearly nine years — found that semaglutide use was associated with a 36% lower risk of alcohol-related hospitalisation compared to periods off the medication. Liraglutide, an older GLP-1 medication, showed a 28% reduction.

The scale and duration of this study make it hard to dismiss. This wasn't a self-selected group of motivated patients — it was population-level data from a national registry, tracked over nearly a decade.

Does it work for other addictions too?

The alcohol findings are the most developed, but there are signals across other addictive behaviours. The JAMA Psychiatry trial showed reduced smoking in participants who smoked. Observational data has suggested associations with reduced cannabis use disorder. And patients on GLP-1 medications routinely report a broad quieting of compulsive urges — gambling, shopping, social media — though controlled trials for these specific behaviours haven't been done.

The biological rationale supports this. If GLP-1 medications work by dampening dopamine-driven reward responses in general, you'd expect the effect to extend beyond any single substance. Whether the clinical evidence will follow is one of the more fascinating open questions in addiction research right now.

What existing treatments are available — and why this matters

Alcohol use disorder is one of the leading causes of preventable death globally, yet the treatment options remain limited. Only three medications are currently approved for AUD in most countries — naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram — and uptake is low. Many people with AUD never receive any pharmacological treatment at all.

The prospect of a medication already widely prescribed for obesity and diabetes also demonstrating meaningful effects on alcohol use is significant for a few reasons. It could reach people who are already on GLP-1 medications for other reasons, it carries less stigma than seeking treatment specifically for AUD, and if the effect sizes hold up in Phase 3 trials, it could represent the most effective pharmacological option for AUD seen in years.

The caveat is cost. Semaglutide is expensive, and access is already constrained for its approved indications. If it gains an AUD indication, questions about prescribing, access, and insurance coverage will be significant.

What the Phase 3 trials will tell us

Phase 3 trials evaluating semaglutide specifically for alcohol use disorder are now underway. These will be substantially larger than the 48-person JAMA Psychiatry trial — typically Phase 3 trials enrol hundreds or thousands of participants — and will be designed to give definitive answers about efficacy, optimal dosing, and safety in this population.

Phase 3 trials will also need to address questions the early research couldn't answer: How long do effects last? What happens when you stop taking the medication? Does the optimal dose for AUD differ from the doses used for obesity? Does it work differently in people who are also trying to abstain completely versus reduce their drinking?

Results from these trials are still likely years away. But the early signal is strong enough that multiple research groups are investing in answering these questions.

What this means if you're currently on a GLP-1 medication

If you've noticed that your drinking has dropped since starting Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or another GLP-1 medication — you're not imagining it, and you're not alone. This is a documented effect that researchers are actively studying. The JAMA Psychiatry trial essentially confirmed what patients had been reporting anecdotally for years.

A few practical points worth knowing:

  • The effect isn't guaranteed. Not everyone on GLP-1 medications notices reduced alcohol cravings. The trial showed an average reduction across a group, not a universal effect in every participant.
  • These medications aren't approved for AUD. If you have concerns about your drinking, speak to your doctor. There are established treatments available, and a reduction in cravings from your GLP-1 medication could be a useful starting point for that conversation.
  • The mechanism may not be purely direct. Some of the reduction in drinking could reflect broader lifestyle changes — eating better, feeling healthier, being more mindful of health generally. Separating the neurological effect from these indirect effects is difficult.
  • Don't use alcohol to manage GLP-1 side effects. Some people drink to manage nausea from GLP-1 medications. This is worth discussing with your doctor — there are better options.

The bottom line

The evidence that GLP-1 medications reduce alcohol cravings and consumption is now substantial enough to take seriously. It spans a randomised controlled trial, large real-world datasets, a plausible and well-studied biological mechanism, and consistent patient reports. This isn't a rumour — it's an emerging clinical finding.

What we don't yet have is the Phase 3 evidence needed to formally approve these medications for alcohol use disorder, or the long-term data to know how durable the effects are. But the research is moving quickly, and the next few years should provide much clearer answers.

For the broader GLP-1 story — medications developed to lower blood sugar that turned out to treat obesity, reduce cardiovascular risk, and now show promise for addiction — alcohol use disorder may be one more chapter in what is becoming a remarkably wide-ranging pharmacological story.
