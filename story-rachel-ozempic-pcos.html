Ozempic · PCOS

"Ozempic Didn't Just Help My Weight — It Helped My PCOS"

Jan 20, 2026·9 min read
At a glance: Rachel S., 31, was prescribed Ozempic for weight management. She'd also been living with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) since her early twenties — irregular periods, hormonal acne, insulin resistance, and years of frustration. Within months of starting Ozempic, her PCOS symptoms began improving in ways she never expected. Here's what happened.

Living with PCOS for a decade

I was diagnosed with PCOS at 21. If you're not familiar with it, polycystic ovary syndrome is a hormonal condition that affects roughly 1 in 10 women of reproductive age. For me, it meant irregular periods (sometimes going four or five months without one), persistent hormonal acne along my jawline and chin, difficulty losing weight despite genuinely trying, and insulin resistance that my GP kept telling me would eventually become type 2 diabetes if I didn't get it under control.

Over the years I tried everything my doctors suggested. Metformin for the insulin resistance. The combined pill to regulate my periods. Spironolactone for the acne. Each one helped a bit, but nothing ever addressed the whole picture. It always felt like I was playing whack-a-mole with my symptoms.

PCOS is exhausting because it touches everything — your skin, your weight, your hormones, your fertility, your mood. There's no single treatment that fixes it. You just manage, manage, manage.

The weight piece was the most demoralising. PCOS makes it significantly harder to lose weight — the insulin resistance creates a cycle where your body stores fat more easily and burns it less efficiently. I'd do everything "right" — calorie deficit, regular exercise — and lose a pound in a month. My friends without PCOS would do the same and lose five. It was infuriating.

Starting Ozempic

My endocrinologist suggested Ozempic in mid-2025. Her primary reasoning was weight management and improving my insulin resistance — she was worried about my fasting insulin levels and said I was "on the doorstep" of a diabetes diagnosis. The weight loss would be a bonus, but the metabolic improvement was the real goal.

She didn't mention PCOS specifically when prescribing it. At that point, I don't think she was expecting it to help with those symptoms directly. The hope was that losing weight would indirectly improve my hormonal profile — which is the standard line for PCOS patients. Lose weight, and the hormones often follow.

I started on 0.25mg and titrated up over the first couple of months. Side effects were manageable — mild nausea for the first three weeks, some constipation that I managed with extra water and fibre. By month two on the 0.5mg dose, I felt settled.

The changes I didn't expect

My period came back

This was the first sign that something beyond weight loss was happening. I got my period at week eight on Ozempic. That might not sound remarkable, but at that point I hadn't had a natural period in almost four months. I'd been so used to irregular cycles that I'd stopped tracking them entirely.

Then it came again four weeks later. And again four weeks after that. By month five, I had my first three consecutive regular cycles since I was a teenager.

I cried. I'm not someone who cries easily, but sitting in my bathroom realising that my body was doing something normally — something it hadn't done in a decade — was overwhelming. It felt like my body was finally working the way it was supposed to.

The acne cleared

My hormonal acne had been a constant companion since my early twenties. Deep, painful cysts along my jawline and chin that would take weeks to heal and leave marks. I'd tried topical retinoids, antibiotics, spironolactone — they helped but never fully cleared it. I'd accepted it as just part of having PCOS.

Around month three on Ozempic, I noticed I wasn't getting new breakouts. By month five, my skin was the clearest it had been since I was 19. I don't mean slightly better — I mean clear. No active spots. No painful cysts. Just normal skin.

My dermatologist was genuinely surprised at my next appointment. She said the improvement was likely driven by reduced insulin levels — high insulin stimulates androgen production, which drives the hormonal acne cycle in PCOS. When the insulin came down, the androgens followed, and the acne resolved.

My insulin resistance had essentially normalised. My testosterone — which had been elevated, driving the acne and hair growth — had dropped into the normal range. My SHBG (a protein that binds excess hormones) had more than doubled, which meant less free testosterone circulating in my system. My A1C was no longer prediabetic.

My endocrinologist called it "one of the best responses she'd seen." She said the metabolic improvement was driving the hormonal improvement, which was driving the symptom improvement. It was a cascade — and Ozempic had triggered it by tackling the insulin resistance at the root.

Why GLP-1 medications can help PCOS: PCOS and insulin resistance are closely linked. Up to 70% of women with PCOS have some degree of insulin resistance, which drives excess androgen production (testosterone and similar hormones). These excess androgens cause many of the hallmark PCOS symptoms — irregular periods, acne, excess hair growth, and difficulty losing weight. By improving insulin sensitivity and promoting weight loss, GLP-1 medications can break this cycle. Ozempic is not currently approved specifically for PCOS, but emerging research suggests it may be particularly beneficial for PCOS patients with insulin resistance.

The fertility question

I need to address this because it's the first thing people ask when I tell them my periods came back: "So are you going to try for a baby?"

It's complicated. My partner and I had been told that conceiving naturally would be difficult with my PCOS. We'd had preliminary conversations about fertility treatment. We hadn't ruled out children, but we'd parked the conversation because it felt overwhelming on top of everything else.

Now that my cycles are regular and my hormonal profile has improved, the conversation has reopened. My endocrinologist was clear: Ozempic is not approved for use during pregnancy, and the recommendation is to stop it at least two months before trying to conceive. But the metabolic improvements I've gained while on it could persist for some time after stopping — and regular ovulation is a prerequisite for natural conception.

We're not trying yet. But the fact that it's now a realistic possibility rather than a distant long-shot is something I never expected Ozempic to give me.

Important note on fertility and GLP-1 medications: GLP-1 medications are not approved for use during pregnancy and should be discontinued before attempting to conceive. Some women on GLP-1 medications have reported unexpected pregnancies, possibly due to restored ovulation. If you have PCOS and are taking a GLP-1 medication, talk to your doctor about contraception — especially if you're not planning a pregnancy. Improved fertility can be an unexpected side effect.

What hasn't changed

I want to be balanced about this. Ozempic has been remarkable for me, but it hasn't cured my PCOS. I still have polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. I still have some excess hair growth, though it's slowed noticeably. If I stop Ozempic, my endocrinologist says there's a real possibility that the insulin resistance and hormonal imbalances could return — especially if I regain weight.

PCOS is a chronic condition. Ozempic is managing it, not curing it. That distinction matters. I'm in a much better place than I was a year ago, but I'm not under any illusion that I'm "fixed."

Ten months in — where I am now

I'm on 1.0mg Ozempic weekly. I've lost 34 lbs total. My periods have been regular for seven consecutive months. My skin is clear. My A1C is normal. My mood is better — partly because of the physical improvements, and partly because I finally feel like my body is cooperating with me instead of fighting me at every turn.

My endocrinologist has discontinued my metformin and reduced my spironolactone. I'm on fewer medications now than I have been at any point since my PCOS diagnosis. That alone feels like a victory.

What I'd tell other women with PCOS

  • Ask about GLP-1 medications specifically. Not all doctors are thinking about GLP-1s in the context of PCOS yet. If you have PCOS with insulin resistance, it's worth raising the conversation, even if your doctor hasn't suggested it.
  • Get a full hormonal panel before and after. The numbers tell the story. Without bloodwork, you're guessing. Fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, total and free testosterone, SHBG — these are the markers that show whether the underlying hormonal picture is shifting.
  • Track your cycles. I regret not tracking mine more carefully before starting Ozempic. Having baseline data makes the improvement much clearer — and it's useful for your doctor to see.
  • Be aware of the fertility implications. If your periods return and you're not planning a pregnancy, make sure your contraception is sorted. Restored ovulation is a real possibility, and some women have been caught off guard by it.
  • Don't expect a cure. Expect management. Good management, potentially excellent management, but not a cure. PCOS is chronic. Ozempic is a tool, not an endpoint.
  • It's not just about weight. The weight loss matters, but the metabolic improvement underneath it matters more. Even women who lose a modest amount of weight on GLP-1 medications can see significant hormonal improvements. It's the insulin sensitivity, not just the scale, that drives the PCOS benefits.
Research to watch: Several clinical trials are currently investigating GLP-1 medications specifically for PCOS management. Early results are promising, suggesting benefits for ovulation, androgen levels, and metabolic health in women with PCOS — even independent of weight loss. This is an area of active research, and it's likely we'll see more specific guidance for PCOS patients in the coming years.

What Ozempic gave me back

I started Ozempic hoping to lose weight and avoid a diabetes diagnosis. Those things happened. But what I didn't expect was getting my body back in a much broader sense — regular periods for the first time in a decade, clear skin, normal bloodwork, and a realistic conversation about fertility that I'd all but given up on.

For ten years, PCOS felt like a condition I was losing to. Everything I tried helped a little but never enough. Ozempic didn't just help a little. It changed the whole picture. And for the first time in a very long time, I feel like I'm managing my PCOS rather than my PCOS managing me.
