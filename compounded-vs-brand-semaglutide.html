Compounded Semaglutide vs. Brand Name: What You Need to Know

Updated Feb 2026·13 min read

For the past two years, millions of Americans used compounded semaglutide as an affordable alternative to Ozempic and Wegovy. At $100–300 per month versus $1,000+, compounded versions made GLP-1 treatment accessible to people who couldn't otherwise afford it.

That era is now effectively over. The FDA declared the semaglutide shortage resolved in February 2025, and as of mid-2025, compounding pharmacies can no longer legally produce copies of these medications. But the questions people have about compounded versus brand-name semaglutide haven't gone away — especially as some providers still offer compounded products in legally grey areas.

Here's a clear-eyed look at what happened, what the differences actually are, and what your options look like now.

What is compounded semaglutide?

Compounding is when a pharmacy creates a customised medication for an individual patient. It's a legitimate part of medicine — for instance, a pharmacist might compound a liquid version of a drug for a child who can't swallow pills. Compounding pharmacies operate under Section 503A or 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

Compounded semaglutide was produced by these pharmacies using bulk semaglutide ingredient sourced from chemical suppliers. The finished product was typically provided in multi-dose vials that patients would draw from with a syringe — different from the convenient pre-filled pens used for Ozempic and Wegovy.

Some compounders also offered alternative forms like sublingual drops, dissolving tablets, and nasal sprays. None of these alternative delivery methods have been studied in clinical trials, and how much semaglutide the body actually absorbs from these forms is unknown.

Why compounding was allowed (and why it isn't now)

Under federal law, compounding pharmacies are normally prohibited from producing copies of commercially available FDA-approved drugs. There's an exception, though: when an FDA-approved drug is in shortage, compounders can step in to fill the gap.

That's exactly what happened with semaglutide. Demand for Ozempic and Wegovy exploded starting in 2022, far exceeding Novo Nordisk's manufacturing capacity. The FDA declared an official shortage, which opened the door for compounding pharmacies to legally produce semaglutide.

The market grew quickly. With the ingredient cost estimated at under $5 per month of supply, compounders could sell at $100–300 per month and still earn substantial margins. For patients facing $1,000+ brand-name prices, it was transformative.

Then the shortage ended. The FDA declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved in December 2024, and the semaglutide shortage resolved in February 2025. The legal basis for mass compounding disappeared. The deadline for pharmacies to stop producing compounded tirzepatide passed in March 2025, and compounded semaglutide followed in May 2025.

The safety concerns were real

The appeal of compounded semaglutide was understandable, but the safety concerns raised by the FDA and medical professionals weren't just industry talking points. Several genuine issues emerged over the two-year compounding era.

Dosing errors

The FDA received multiple reports of adverse events related to dosing mistakes with compounded semaglutide, some requiring hospitalisation. Unlike pre-filled pens with fixed doses, compounded products came in vials of varying concentrations. Patients had to measure their own doses using syringes, and converting between milligrams and volume units led to errors. Some compounders offered multiple concentrations, adding further confusion.

Ingredient quality

Research found that some compounded samples contained impurities, unapproved inactive ingredients, and in some cases banned substances. The active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) was predominantly sourced from overseas suppliers with their own quality definitions rather than the standardised manufacturing processes used by Novo Nordisk.

Salt form substitution

The FDA identified that some compounders were using salt forms of semaglutide — specifically semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate — rather than semaglutide base. These are chemically distinct molecules from what's in Ozempic and Wegovy. The salt forms have never been evaluated by the FDA for safety or effectiveness, and the body may absorb and process them differently.

Unproven formulations

Sublingual drops, dissolving tablets, nasal sprays, and other alternative delivery methods were marketed without any clinical evidence. The absorption rate of semaglutide through these routes hasn't been studied in humans. Patients using these forms had no way of knowing how much active medication was actually reaching their bloodstream.

Fraudulent products

The FDA identified outright fraudulent products — vials labelled with the names of compounding pharmacies that didn't actually produce them, or pharmacies that didn't exist at all. At least one adverse event was reported from a product with a fabricated pharmacy label.

To be clear: Many patients had positive experiences with compounded semaglutide from reputable pharmacies. The point isn't that every compounded product was dangerous — it's that the system lacked the safeguards to consistently ensure quality, and the bad actors were difficult for patients to identify.

Where things stand legally in 2026

The legal landscape has shifted decisively against compounded GLP-1 medications.

With no active shortage, pharmacies cannot legally produce copies of Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound. The FDA announced in February 2026 that it would take further action to restrict access to GLP-1 ingredients for non-FDA approved compounded drugs. Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly have both aggressively pursued legal action against compounders, telehealth companies, and medspas still marketing compounded products.

The National Advertising Division (NAD) has issued multiple opinions ordering companies to stop making claims that imply compounded versions are equivalent to FDA-approved products. The FDA published over 55 warning letters to online sellers in September 2025 alone.

Some 503A compounding pharmacies continue to offer semaglutide in limited circumstances — for genuinely patient-specific needs such as documented allergies to inactive ingredients in the brand-name product. But mass compounding for weight loss is no longer legal.

If you encounter a provider still offering compounded semaglutide without a very specific clinical justification, proceed with extreme caution. They may be operating outside the law, and the products carry additional risk now that the legitimate supply chain has been disrupted.

What this means if you were using compounded semaglutide

If you were one of the millions of patients using compounded GLP-1 medications, you have several options — and they're considerably better than they were even six months ago.

Brand-name prices have dropped significantly

The competition from compounders accomplished something lasting: it forced manufacturers to lower prices. Self-pay options now include Wegovy oral tablets starting at $149/month, Ozempic at $199–349/month through NovoCare, and Zepbound at $299–449/month through LillyDirect. TrumpRx.gov offers further discounts. These are a far cry from the $1,000+ list prices that drove people to compounding in the first place.

Insurance coverage is expanding

Medicare now covers GLP-1 medications for obesity at a $50 monthly copay. Nearly half of large employers cover weight-loss medications. Manufacturer savings cards bring insured patients' costs as low as $25/month. Check our full guide to getting GLP-1 medications for under $300/month.

Generic liraglutide is available

Teva's generic liraglutide (the same active ingredient as Saxenda) is the first generic GLP-1 available. It requires daily injections and produces more modest weight loss than semaglutide, but it's a legitimate, FDA-reviewed option at a lower price point.

Talk to your doctor about transitioning

If you've been on compounded semaglutide and need to switch to a brand-name product, your doctor can help determine the equivalent dose. The transition is generally straightforward since the active ingredient is the same (assuming your compounded product used semaglutide base rather than a salt form). Don't try to stockpile compounded product or switch to an unverified online source.

The bigger picture

The compounded semaglutide story reveals a genuine tension in healthcare. The FDA's position — that patients should use rigorously tested, quality-controlled medications — is grounded in real safety concerns. But the reason compounding exploded wasn't because patients were reckless. It was because effective medications were priced beyond their reach.

The positive outcome is that market pressure from compounders, combined with government negotiation, has permanently shifted the pricing landscape. GLP-1 medications are still expensive, but the era of $1,300/month as the only option is over. The challenge going forward is ensuring that the patients who benefited from affordable compounded access don't fall through the cracks as the market transitions to brand-name-only supply.
