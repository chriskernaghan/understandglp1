Long-Term · Saxenda

"2 Years on Saxenda: The Honest Truth About What Happens Long-Term"

Feb 3, 2026·14 min read
At a glance: Maria L., 44, has been on Saxenda (liraglutide) for two full years. She lost 51 lbs in the first year — then hit a plateau that lasted five months. She's battled insurance denials, daily injection fatigue, and the question everyone on a GLP-1 eventually faces: do I stay on this forever? Here's the unfiltered version of what long-term GLP-1 use actually looks like.

Starting Saxenda — before the newer drugs existed

I started Saxenda in early 2024. At the time, Ozempic was already making headlines but it was nearly impossible to get — constant shortages, long waiting lists, and my insurance wouldn't cover it for weight management. Wegovy was even harder to find.

My endocrinologist suggested Saxenda instead. It was available, it was covered by my insurance (at the time), and it had years of clinical data behind it. It wasn't the shiny new thing, but it was proven. I was 42, my BMI was 38, and I had prediabetes that was getting worse. I needed something that actually worked.

The catch with Saxenda: it's a daily injection. Not weekly like Ozempic or Mounjaro. Every single day. My doctor was upfront about that being the biggest downside. She also explained that the weight loss tends to be more modest than what you see with the newer medications. I decided I'd rather have something available now than wait indefinitely for something theoretically better.

About Saxenda: Saxenda (liraglutide) is a GLP-1 receptor agonist made by Novo Nordisk — the same company that makes Ozempic and Wegovy. It was approved for weight management in 2014, making it one of the earlier GLP-1 medications for obesity. The active ingredient (liraglutide) is the same as in Victoza, which is used for type 2 diabetes. The key difference from newer GLP-1s: Saxenda requires a daily injection rather than a weekly one, and the average weight loss in clinical trials was around 8% of body weight.

Year one: the honeymoon period

The titration took five weeks — you start at 0.6mg and increase by 0.6mg each week until you reach the full 3.0mg dose. The first few weeks were rough. Nausea was significant, especially at the 1.8mg and 2.4mg steps. I lost my appetite almost completely at certain points, which sounds like it should be a good thing, but when you feel genuinely ill it's not.

By week six or seven, the side effects settled. And then Saxenda started doing what it was supposed to do.

The first six months were incredible. The weight was coming off steadily, my prediabetes markers were improving, and for the first time in years I felt like I had some control. I thought I'd cracked it.

I lost 28 lbs in the first six months. By the end of year one, I was down 51 lbs. My A1C had dropped from 6.3 (prediabetic range) to 5.6 (normal). My blood pressure improved. My doctor reduced my cholesterol medication. On paper and in practice, everything was working.

The daily injection reality

I want to be honest about the daily injection, because it's the thing people ask about most. In the beginning, it wasn't a problem. I was motivated, the weight was coming off, and the injection itself is quick and mostly painless — a tiny needle, takes five seconds.

But after a few months, the novelty wears off and it just becomes another thing you have to do. Every single day. You can't skip a day and double up. You can't forget. If you're travelling, the pen comes with you. If you're out late and forgot to do it before dinner, you're injecting in a restaurant bathroom.

By month eight or nine, I started to feel what I can only describe as injection fatigue. Not physical fatigue — the injection itself is fine. Mental fatigue. The daily-ness of it. The fact that it never stops. It's a small thing that takes up a surprisingly large amount of mental space.

The plateau

Around month 13, the weight loss stopped. Completely. I was still taking Saxenda every day, still eating the same way, still doing my walks. The scale didn't move for five months.

This was the hardest part of the entire two years.

Nobody prepares you for the plateau. You're still injecting every day, still doing everything right, and the scale just stops. You start wondering if the medication has stopped working — or if you've somehow failed again.

My doctor reassured me that plateaus are normal and expected. She explained that the body adapts — your metabolism adjusts to your new weight, your caloric needs decrease, and the same medication dose that produced weight loss at a higher weight now just maintains at a lower one. She said the fact that I wasn't regaining was actually the medication still working.

That reframing helped, but it didn't make it easy. When you're injecting yourself every day and nothing is happening, it's hard not to question whether it's worth it.

What I tried during the plateau

  • Increased protein intake: My doctor suggested I might be under-eating protein (a common theme with GLP-1 patients, apparently). I bumped my intake to around 90g per day and noticed my energy improved, even if the scale didn't immediately respond.
  • Added resistance training: I'd been walking consistently but not doing any strength work. I started two gym sessions a week. The scale still didn't move much, but my clothes started fitting differently — my body was changing shape even if my weight wasn't.
  • Accepted maintenance as a win: This was the hardest shift. After a year of steady loss, I had to accept that holding steady at minus 51 lbs was not a failure. It was the medication doing its job — just a different job than before.

The insurance battle

Six months into Saxenda, my insurance changed their coverage policy. They'd been covering it — then suddenly, they weren't. No warning, no transition period. My pharmacy called to tell me my next prescription would cost $1,400 out of pocket.

What followed was three months of appeals, prior authorisations, letters from my doctor, and phone calls that went nowhere. My endocrinologist wrote a detailed letter explaining that Saxenda had reversed my prediabetes and that discontinuing it risked weight regain and metabolic deterioration. The insurance company denied the first appeal. And the second.

My doctor's office helped me apply for Novo Nordisk's patient assistance programme. That eventually came through and brought my cost down to a manageable copay. But those three months of uncertainty were incredibly stressful. I was rationing my remaining pens, stretching them further than I should have, and constantly worried about what would happen if I had to stop.

If you're facing an insurance denial: Don't accept the first rejection. Ask your doctor to submit a prior authorisation with clinical documentation — A1C results, weight history, comorbidity records. If that's denied, file a formal appeal. Many pharmaceutical companies also have patient assistance programmes for people who've lost coverage. It's a frustrating process, but persistence often pays off.

The "should I switch?" question

About 18 months in, Wegovy became more widely available and Mounjaro was getting a lot of attention. My doctor and I had the inevitable conversation: should I switch to a newer, more potent GLP-1?

The arguments for switching were real. The newer medications produce greater average weight loss. They're weekly instead of daily. The clinical data is impressive. On paper, switching made sense.

But there were arguments for staying too:

  • Saxenda was working for me. My prediabetes was reversed. I'd lost 51 lbs and kept it off. My health markers were all improved.
  • I knew my side effect profile. I tolerated Saxenda well. Switching to a new medication meant restarting titration and potentially dealing with a whole new set of side effects.
  • Insurance was finally stable. My coverage for Saxenda was sorted. Switching medications meant another round of prior authorisations and potential denials.
  • The plateau had broken. Around month 18, I started losing again — slowly, but moving. The resistance training seemed to be making a difference.

We decided to stay on Saxenda. My doctor's view was that the best GLP-1 medication is the one that's working, that you can tolerate, and that you can access. Chasing a theoretically better option when you're doing well on your current one isn't always the right call.

Two years in — where I am now

6 months -28 lbs 5.8 Steady loss, side effects settled
12 months -51 lbs 5.6 Best results period
13–18 months -51 lbs (plateau) 5.6 No loss for 5 months; added strength training
24 months (now) -58 lbs 5.5 Slow loss resumed; body recomposition visible

I'm down 58 lbs total. My A1C is 5.5 — solidly normal. My prediabetes is gone. I'm off cholesterol medication. My blood pressure is managed with a lower dose than before. Physically, I'm in the best shape I've been in since my early 30s.

I still inject every day. Some days I don't even think about it — it's just what I do after brushing my teeth in the morning. Other days, especially when I'm tired or travelling, it feels like a burden. The daily frequency is Saxenda's biggest weakness, and I'd be lying if I said it didn't bother me sometimes.

What two years has taught me

  • Long-term GLP-1 use is a different experience than starting one. The first six months are dramatic — rapid changes, noticeable results, high motivation. After that, it becomes about maintenance, patience, and redefining success. Nobody talks about this enough.
  • Plateaus are not failures. They're your body adapting. The medication is still working — it's just working to maintain rather than to lose. That's still valuable. That's still the medication doing its job.
  • Insurance is an ongoing battle. If you're in the US, assume your coverage will be challenged at some point and have a plan. Patient assistance programmes exist. Your doctor can advocate for you. But you have to be prepared to fight for it.
  • Daily injections are manageable but not trivial. Anyone who tells you it's "no big deal" hasn't done 730 of them. It's fine. It's manageable. But it takes up mental space, and it's okay to acknowledge that.
  • The newer drugs aren't always the answer. Saxenda is older, less potent on average, and requires daily dosing. It also reversed my prediabetes, helped me lose 58 lbs, and has been stable and tolerable for two years. Don't overlook a medication because it isn't the one making headlines.
  • This is a long-term commitment. My doctor has been clear: if I stop Saxenda, there's a significant likelihood of weight regain. That's not a scare tactic — it's the reality of how these medications work. I've made peace with the idea that this might be something I do indefinitely, and that's okay. People take blood pressure medication for life. This isn't fundamentally different.
Thinking about Saxenda? Saxenda may not be the newest GLP-1 on the market, but it has the longest real-world track record for weight management. If newer medications are unavailable, unaffordable, or not tolerated, Saxenda remains a legitimate and effective option. Talk to your doctor about whether it's the right fit for your situation.

What's next for me

I'm going to stay on Saxenda for now. My doctor and I review every six months. If the medication stops being effective, or if a weekly version of liraglutide becomes available, or if my insurance situation changes, we'll reassess.

The truth about long-term GLP-1 use is that it's less dramatic and more mundane than the headlines suggest. It's not a miracle. It's a tool — one that requires daily commitment, occasional frustration, and the patience to ride out the parts where nothing seems to be happening. But two years in, with 58 lbs gone and my prediabetes reversed, I'd make the same choice again without hesitation.
