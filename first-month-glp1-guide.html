Getting Started

Your First Month on a GLP-1: A Week-by-Week Guide

Updated Feb 2026·11 min read

Starting a GLP-1 medication is a big step, and the first month is when most of the adjustment happens. Your body is adapting to a new hormone signal, your appetite is shifting, and you're navigating side effects that can range from barely noticeable to genuinely miserable.

The good news: nearly everything you'll experience in these first four weeks is temporary. Most side effects peak early and improve as your body adjusts. Knowing what to expect — and what's actually normal — makes the whole process far less stressful.

This guide covers semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound). The timelines are broadly similar, though tirzepatide tends to produce appetite suppression slightly faster for some patients.

Before your first injection

A little preparation goes a long way. In the days before you start, there are a few things worth doing.

Stock your kitchen. You're going to want easy, protein-rich foods that require minimal cooking and don't have strong smells. Greek yoghurt, eggs, cottage cheese, protein powder, pre-cooked chicken, crackers, ginger tea, and clear broths are all good to have on hand. See our high-protein breakfast ideas for specific suggestions.

Take baseline measurements. Weigh yourself, take body measurements if you want to (waist, hips, chest), and consider a photo. Weight can fluctuate wildly in the first month, so having a baseline helps you see the bigger picture later. Some people also note their baseline energy levels, mood, and appetite to track changes.

Choose your injection day wisely. Many patients recommend doing your first injection on a Friday evening or Saturday morning. If side effects hit, you have the weekend to rest without missing work. Once you know how you react, you can adjust your injection day going forward.

Read the injection instructions. Whether you're using a pre-filled pen or a vial, watch the manufacturer's instruction video before your first dose. The injection itself is straightforward — a small needle into the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm — but nerves can make the first one feel bigger than it is. It takes about 5 seconds.

Starting doses are deliberately low. Ozempic starts at 0.25mg, Wegovy at 0.25mg, Mounjaro at 2.5mg, and Zepbound at 2.5mg. These doses are sub-therapeutic — they're not designed to produce significant weight loss. They exist to let your body adapt before you move to effective doses. Don't be discouraged if nothing dramatic happens in weeks 1–2.

Week 1

Days 1–7 · The adjustment begins

What to expect

Many people feel very little in the first week. The starting dose is low, and it takes several days for the medication to reach steady levels in your body. Some patients notice a mild reduction in appetite by day 3–4, while others feel no different at all. Both responses are completely normal.

The most common early side effects are mild nausea (not the vomiting kind — more like a faint queasiness), slight fatigue, and occasionally a headache. These tend to appear within 24–48 hours of the injection and may last 2–3 days before fading.

What to do

Eat normally but mindfully. Don't try to restrict your intake in week 1. Eat when you're hungry, stop when you're full, and notice how your appetite feels compared to before. Some patients experience their first encounter with reduced "food noise" — the constant background hum of thinking about food goes quieter.

Stay hydrated. Aim for at least 2 litres of water per day from the start. Dehydration amplifies every side effect — nausea, headaches, fatigue, constipation. If plain water feels unappealing, try adding lemon, drinking herbal tea, or eating water-rich foods like cucumber and melon.

Don't weigh yourself obsessively. Daily weight can fluctuate by 1–2kg based on water retention, food in your stomach, and dozens of other factors. If you're going to weigh yourself, do it once at the end of the week under the same conditions (morning, after using the bathroom, before eating).

Typical weight change: 0–1kg loss. Some people see nothing; others see a quick initial drop that's mostly water. Neither means anything about your long-term trajectory.

Week 2

Days 8–14 · Side effects may peak, appetite shifts

What to expect

For many patients, week 2 is the trickiest. Your body has now had two doses (if on a weekly injectable) and the medication is building in your system. Nausea often peaks during this period. You may also experience constipation, mild bloating, or a general "full" feeling that lingers longer than usual after meals.

The appetite suppression typically becomes more noticeable. You might find yourself forgetting to eat lunch, losing interest in snacks, or feeling satisfied after half a portion. The "food noise" effect is often more pronounced — patients describe it as the mental chatter about food simply going quiet.

What to do

Eat smaller, more frequent meals. Three big meals may feel overwhelming. Try five smaller ones instead. Your stomach is emptying more slowly than usual, so giving it less at a time reduces nausea and bloating.

Avoid greasy, fried, and very rich foods. These are the number one nausea trigger. Your digestion has slowed, and fatty foods sit like a rock. Lean proteins, simple carbs, and bland foods are your friends this week. Think chicken and rice, toast with eggs, soup, yoghurt.

Ginger helps. Ginger tea, ginger sweets, ginger biscuits — all have evidence for reducing nausea. Keep some handy. Peppermint tea is another option.

Don't skip meals entirely. Even when you're not hungry, try to eat something small and protein-rich every 4–5 hours. Skipping meals entirely can worsen nausea (empty-stomach nausea is real) and leads to even lower calorie and protein intake than is ideal.

Typical weight change: 0.5–1.5kg loss from baseline. Still early days. Some patients see more, some see less.

Week 3

Days 15–21 · The body adapts

What to expect

Week 3 is where most patients turn a corner. Nausea typically starts to improve as your body adjusts to the medication. Your appetite is more consistently suppressed, and you're getting better at reading your new hunger signals. Portions naturally shrink.

Constipation may persist or emerge for the first time. GLP-1 medications slow your entire digestive tract, and with reduced food intake (meaning less fibre), things can get sluggish. This is manageable but worth addressing early before it becomes uncomfortable.

Some patients notice changes in their relationship with food beyond just appetite. Cravings for sweets or high-fat foods may decrease. The compulsive element of eating — eating because it's there, eating out of boredom, eating past fullness — starts to ease. This is one of the most commonly reported benefits and one of the hardest to quantify.

What to do

Focus on protein. Now that the worst of the nausea is (hopefully) easing, this is the week to get serious about protein. Aim for 25–30g at each meal. Your total food intake is lower, which means every bite counts more. Protein first, everything else second. See our full diet guide for the framework.

Address constipation proactively. Increase fibre from vegetables, add a tablespoon of chia seeds to yoghurt or oats, drink plenty of water, and consider a gentle fibre supplement like psyllium husk if needed. Walking also helps keep things moving.

Start moving. If you haven't already, this is a good week to begin some gentle exercise. Walking 20–30 minutes daily is an excellent start. If you're feeling good, consider beginning a strength training programme — the earlier you start, the better your muscle preservation will be.

Typical weight change: 1–2.5kg loss from baseline. The trend should be clearly downward by now, even if individual days fluctuate.

Week 4

Days 22–28 · Finding your rhythm

What to expect

By week 4, you're starting to settle into a new normal. Most patients have adapted to the medication well enough that side effects are mild or manageable. Your eating patterns have shifted — smaller meals, less snacking, more awareness of fullness cues. The novelty has worn off and the medication is simply part of your routine.

Weight loss at this point is typically modest — remember, you're still on the starting dose. The real weight loss acceleration begins when you titrate up to higher doses over the coming months. Semaglutide patients usually move to 0.5mg at week 4 or 5; tirzepatide patients move to 5mg at week 5.

Some patients feel disappointed by the scale at this point. They've heard stories of people losing 5kg in the first month and feel behind. Don't compare. Starting-dose weight loss varies enormously and is not predictive of long-term results. The dose escalation over the next 2–4 months is where the significant weight loss happens.

What to do

Take stock. Compare your measurements and photos to your baseline. Look at how your eating has changed, how your energy feels, whether your relationship with food has shifted. The scale tells one story; these other indicators often tell a more encouraging one.

Prepare for dose escalation. Your doctor will likely increase your dose at your next appointment. Some patients experience a brief return of side effects when moving up — a day or two of nausea that's usually milder than the initial round. The same strategies that worked in week 2 will work again.

Build sustainable habits now. The habits you establish this month — eating protein first, staying hydrated, exercising regularly, eating smaller meals — are the same habits that will sustain your results at higher doses and eventually when (if) you discontinue the medication. The first month is about more than just weight loss; it's about rewiring your daily patterns.

Typical weight change: 1.5–3kg loss from baseline on starting doses. Some patients see more, particularly those with higher starting weights.

When to call your doctor

Most first-month side effects are normal and manageable. However, contact your doctor if you experience any of the following:

  • Persistent vomiting (not occasional nausea — actual vomiting that prevents you from keeping food or water down for more than 24 hours)
  • Severe abdominal pain that doesn't resolve, particularly in the upper abdomen (this can indicate pancreatitis, which is rare but serious)
  • Signs of dehydration — dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth, rapid heartbeat, inability to drink fluids
  • Allergic reaction — rash, itching, swelling at the injection site that spreads, difficulty breathing
  • Significant mood changes — including new or worsening depression or anxiety. While GLP-1 medications are being studied for potential mental health benefits, some patients do experience negative mood shifts
  • Severe constipation — no bowel movement for 4+ days despite fibre, water, and gentle laxatives

Don't tough it out if something feels wrong. Your doctor can adjust your dose, change your titration schedule, or prescribe anti-nausea medication to help you through the adjustment period.

The bigger picture

The first month is the hardest month. After this, your body has adjusted to the medication, your eating patterns have shifted, and each dose increase is easier than the first. Most patients describe months 2–4 as the period where everything clicks — the side effects are manageable, the weight loss accelerates, and the lifestyle changes start to feel natural rather than forced.

Be patient with yourself. Be patient with the process. And remember that the starting dose is just the beginning.

This guide covers general patterns observed across GLP-1 medications. Individual experiences vary significantly. Always follow your prescribing doctor's specific instructions regarding dosing and titration schedules.
