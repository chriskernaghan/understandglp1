Strength Training on GLP-1: Why It's Non-Negotiable

Updated Feb 2026·8 min read

Here's a number that should get your attention: in clinical trials, 25–40% of the total weight lost on GLP-1 medications was lean body mass — not fat. That includes muscle, which is the tissue you most need to preserve for long-term health, metabolism, and quality of life.

The good news is that this doesn't have to happen. A 2025 prospective study of 200 adults on semaglutide and tirzepatide found that those who followed a resistance training programme and maintained adequate protein intake lost approximately 13% of their body weight but only about 3% of their muscle mass. That's a dramatically better ratio than what the clinical trials showed.

The message from researchers is consistent and clear: combining GLP-1 medications with structured strength training and higher protein intake can substantially mitigate muscle loss. As one obesity medicine specialist put it, nothing will or should replace a focus on resistance exercise.

Why GLP-1 medications cause muscle loss

When your body is in a significant caloric deficit — and GLP-1 medications create exactly that — it doesn't only burn fat for energy. It also breaks down protein from muscle tissue. This isn't unique to GLP-1 drugs; the same thing happens with any substantial weight loss, whether from surgery, dieting, or medication.

However, GLP-1 medications may add a few extra factors. Research suggests these drugs can shift food preferences away from protein-rich foods, meaning patients naturally eat less of the very nutrient that protects muscle. The appetite suppression is so effective that many patients drop to very low calorie intakes without realising it, accelerating the deficit beyond what's ideal for body composition.

A 2024 analysis published in Circulation examined this question in detail and concluded that, when measured properly using MRI rather than cruder methods, the muscle changes observed with GLP-1 treatment appear to be largely adaptive — meaning the body is adjusting proportionally to its new weight rather than experiencing abnormal muscle wasting. But "adaptive" doesn't mean desirable, and it certainly doesn't mean you shouldn't take steps to minimise it.

The real concern: Muscle loss matters beyond aesthetics. Less muscle means a lower metabolic rate (making weight regain more likely), reduced functional strength, higher injury risk, and in older adults, increased risk of frailty and sarcopenia. Preserving muscle isn't vanity — it's the difference between losing weight well and losing weight poorly.

What the research says about strength training + GLP-1s

The evidence base is still catching up to the demand for guidance, but the direction is consistent across multiple studies and expert reviews.

A 2025 review in Frontiers in Clinical Diabetes and Healthcare synthesised the current evidence and concluded that combining GLP-1 receptor agonists with structured lifestyle changes — especially increased protein intake and strength training — can mitigate muscle loss and enhance overall outcomes. The authors argued that future obesity management should prioritise integrated approaches rather than treating medication and lifestyle as separate interventions.

A case series from Texas Tech University examined GLP-1 patients who implemented resistance training and protein intakes of 1.6–2.3g per kilogram of fat-free mass. DXA body composition scans showed favourable outcomes — these patients preserved significantly more lean tissue than what clinical trials typically report.

A University of Hong Kong study using genetic data from over 800,000 individuals confirmed that while both lean and fat mass decrease with GLP-1 treatment, fat loss substantially exceeds muscle loss — and the ratio improves further when exercise is part of the equation.

The practical takeaway: you can't completely prevent muscle loss during significant weight loss, but you can reduce it from a major problem to a minor one. Strength training is the most powerful tool for doing that.

How much training do you actually need?

You don't need to become a gym enthusiast. The research points to a surprisingly achievable minimum.

Frequency: 2–3 sessions per week, with at least one rest day between sessions. Two is enough to see meaningful muscle preservation. Three is better if you can manage it.

Duration: 30–45 minutes per session. This isn't endurance training — shorter, focused sessions with adequate rest between sets are more effective than long workouts.

Intensity: The key is progressive overload — gradually increasing the challenge over time, whether through more weight, more repetitions, or more sets. You should be working hard enough that the last 2–3 repetitions of each set feel genuinely difficult. If you could easily do 5 more reps, the weight is too light.

Exercise selection: Prioritise compound movements that work multiple muscle groups simultaneously. Squats, deadlifts, presses, and rows give you far more return on time than isolation exercises like bicep curls. A programme built around 4–6 compound exercises covers your entire body efficiently.

The beginner programme

This programme is designed for someone who has never lifted weights or hasn't trained in a long time. It uses two alternating workouts performed 3 days per week (for example, Monday/Wednesday/Friday). Start with weights that feel manageable and focus on learning the movement patterns before adding load.

Workout A

Goblet squat — 3 sets of 10 reps. Hold a dumbbell at your chest and squat down until your thighs are parallel to the floor. This is the single best exercise for your legs and core.

Dumbbell bench press (or push-ups) — 3 sets of 10 reps. Lie on a bench with a dumbbell in each hand, press up. If you don't have access to a bench, push-ups from the knees or against a wall are a fine starting point.

Seated cable row (or dumbbell row) — 3 sets of 10 reps. Pull a weight toward your torso, squeezing your shoulder blades together. This works your entire back.

Dumbbell shoulder press — 3 sets of 10 reps. Press dumbbells overhead from shoulder height.

Plank — 3 holds of 20–30 seconds. Build up to 45–60 seconds over time.

Workout B

Romanian deadlift — 3 sets of 10 reps. Hold dumbbells in front of your thighs, hinge at the hips and lower them to mid-shin, keeping your back straight. This targets the hamstrings, glutes, and lower back.

Leg press (or lunges) — 3 sets of 10 reps per leg. If using lunges, hold dumbbells at your sides and step forward.

Lat pulldown (or assisted pull-ups) — 3 sets of 10 reps. Pull a bar down to your chest from overhead. This builds your upper back and arms.

Dumbbell chest fly — 3 sets of 12 reps. Lie on a bench, lower dumbbells out to the sides in a wide arc, then bring them back together.

Farmer's carry — 3 walks of 30 metres. Hold heavy dumbbells at your sides and walk. Deceptively simple, incredibly effective for grip, core, and overall strength.

Week-by-week schedule

Week 1: Mon — Workout A, Wed — Workout B, Fri — Workout A

Week 2: Mon — Workout B, Wed — Workout A, Fri — Workout B

Continue alternating. Rest 60–90 seconds between sets. When you can complete all 3 sets of 10 reps comfortably, increase the weight slightly at your next session.

No gym? No problem. You can do an effective programme at home with a set of adjustable dumbbells and a bench. Bodyweight exercises — push-ups, squats, lunges, rows using a sturdy table — are also effective for beginners, though you'll eventually need external resistance to keep progressing.

What about cardio?

Cardio has genuine health benefits — cardiovascular fitness, mood, stress reduction, improved sleep. Walking in particular is excellent and pairs well with GLP-1 treatment. But when it comes to preserving muscle during weight loss, cardio is not a substitute for strength training. Running, cycling, and swimming don't provide the mechanical stimulus your muscles need to resist breakdown.

The ideal combination is strength training 2–3 times per week plus regular walking or light cardio on other days. If you only have time for one type of exercise, choose strength training. You can get cardiovascular benefits from walking daily, but you cannot replicate the muscle-preserving effects of resistance training through any other activity.

Practical tips for training on GLP-1 medications

Time your meals around training. Try to eat a protein-rich meal or snack 1–2 hours before your workout, and another within an hour after. Even something small — a Greek yoghurt, a protein shake, a handful of chicken — is better than training in a fasted state when you're already in a caloric deficit.

Stay hydrated. Dehydration is already common on GLP-1 medications and exercise increases fluid needs. Drink water before, during, and after your workout. If you experience nausea during exercise, sip small amounts frequently rather than drinking a lot at once.

Start lighter than you think you need to. Your energy levels may be lower than normal, especially during dose escalation or the first few months. Beginning with lighter weights and focusing on form prevents injury and builds confidence. You can always add weight next week.

Expect slower progress than pre-medication. You're in a caloric deficit, which means you won't build muscle as quickly as someone eating at maintenance or a surplus. The goal right now isn't muscle growth — it's muscle preservation. Maintaining your current strength levels while losing fat is an excellent outcome.

Don't skip sessions because you feel low-energy. Show up and do a lighter workout if you need to. A reduced session is vastly better than no session. Consistency matters more than intensity.

Track something. Write down the weights and reps you use. Progressive overload only works if you're actually progressing, and you can't progress if you don't know what you did last time. A simple notebook or phone app is all you need.

The protein connection

Strength training and protein intake work together — you need both. Training provides the stimulus that tells your body to preserve muscle; protein provides the building material. Without adequate protein, even a good training programme won't fully protect against muscle loss.

Current recommendations for GLP-1 patients who are strength training suggest 1.2–2.0g of protein per kilogram of body weight, depending on training intensity. Spreading protein intake across 3–4 meals (aiming for 25–40g per meal) is more effective than consuming it all in one sitting. The bottom line

GLP-1 medications are remarkable tools for weight loss. But medication alone produces weight loss that includes a meaningful proportion of muscle. Adding strength training 2–3 times per week — even as a complete beginner — shifts that ratio dramatically in your favour. Combine it with adequate protein, and you're looking at a transformation that improves not just the number on the scale but your actual health, strength, and body composition.

You don't need a personal trainer, a gym membership, or any prior experience. You need a few dumbbells, three sessions a week, and the consistency to keep showing up. Start today.

This article is for general informational purposes. If you have existing injuries, joint problems, or medical conditions that affect your ability to exercise, consult your doctor or a qualified physiotherapist before beginning a strength training programme.
