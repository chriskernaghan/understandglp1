The GLP-1 Diet: What to Eat (and Avoid) for Best Results

Updated Feb 2026·10 min read

GLP-1 medications do the heavy lifting on appetite and cravings — but what you eat with that reduced appetite matters enormously. When you're eating 40–60% less food than before, every meal has to work harder. The wrong choices lead to muscle loss, nutrient deficiencies, fatigue, and worse side effects. The right ones preserve your muscle mass, keep your energy up, and actually make the medication more effective.

This isn't a restrictive diet plan. It's a practical framework for getting the most out of the food you can eat.

The single most important rule: protein first

If you take one thing from this article, let it be this: eat your protein before anything else on your plate.

When your total food intake drops significantly, your body doesn't just burn fat — it also breaks down muscle for energy. Studies on GLP-1 weight loss consistently show that 25–40% of total weight lost can be lean muscle mass. That's a serious problem. Muscle loss lowers your metabolism, reduces your strength, and can leave you looking and feeling worse even at a lower weight.

The fix is protein. Most experts now recommend GLP-1 patients aim for 1.2–1.5g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, or roughly 80–120g for most people. That's considerably more than the general population guidelines, and it's deliberately high because you're in a significant caloric deficit.

Protein targets by body weight: 70kg (155lbs) → aim for 85–105g/day. 90kg (200lbs) → aim for 110–135g/day. 110kg (240lbs) → aim for 130–165g/day. These are targets, not mandates — getting close matters more than hitting the number exactly.

Practically, this means building every meal around a protein source and eating it first. If your appetite runs out halfway through the plate, at least the protein is in. The toast, the rice, the sides — those are secondary.

What to eat: the GLP-1 food priorities

Lean proteins (the foundation)

Chicken breast, turkey, fish (especially salmon, cod, and tuna), prawns, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, lean beef, tofu, and tempeh. These should appear at every meal. Eggs and Greek yoghurt are particularly useful because they're easy to eat even on low-appetite days — no heavy cooking, no strong smells, and they go down smoothly.

Non-starchy vegetables (fill without fullness)

Spinach, broccoli, courgette, peppers, green beans, asparagus, cucumber, lettuce, tomatoes, mushrooms, and cauliflower. These provide vitamins, minerals, and fibre without taking up much of your limited caloric budget. They also help with the constipation that many GLP-1 patients experience. Cooked vegetables are generally easier to tolerate than raw ones on a sensitive stomach.

Complex carbohydrates (in moderation)

Oats, sweet potatoes, brown rice, quinoa, wholegrain bread, and legumes (lentils, chickpeas, black beans). You don't need to avoid carbs, but they should take a back seat to protein. A small portion of complex carbs with each meal provides sustained energy and helps with fibre intake. Avoid filling up on bread or rice before you've eaten your protein.

Healthy fats (small amounts, big impact)

Avocado, olive oil, nuts, seeds, nut butters, and fatty fish. Fats are calorie-dense, which is actually useful when your total intake is low — a tablespoon of olive oil or a quarter of an avocado adds meaningful calories and essential nutrients without much volume. They also help your body absorb fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

Hydrating foods and fluids

Dehydration is a common and underappreciated issue on GLP-1 medications. Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea can deplete fluids quickly, and many patients simply forget to drink because their normal hunger and thirst cues are suppressed. Aim for at least 2 litres of water daily. Foods with high water content — cucumber, watermelon, soups, smoothies — can help bridge the gap. See our hydration guide for more detail.

What to avoid (or reduce)

High-fat, greasy foods

This is the number one dietary trigger for nausea and stomach discomfort on GLP-1 medications. Fried foods, heavy cream sauces, fast food, and very rich meals sit in your stomach much longer because GLP-1s already slow gastric emptying. The combination of a full stomach and slow digestion is a recipe for nausea. If you're going to eat something indulgent, keep the portion very small.

Sugary foods and drinks

Fizzy drinks, sweets, pastries, and fruit juice provide calories without protein or meaningful nutrition. When you can only eat 1,200–1,500 calories a day, spending 300 of them on a sugary coffee drink means 300 fewer calories available for the protein and nutrients your body actually needs. Sugar also tends to spike and crash blood glucose, which can worsen fatigue.

Alcohol

GLP-1 medications change how your body processes alcohol. Many patients report getting intoxicated much faster and experiencing worse hangovers. There are also emerging reports of increased alcohol sensitivity and lower tolerance. Beyond that, alcohol is empty calories — a glass of wine is roughly 150 calories with no protein. If you do drink, keep it occasional and modest, and be aware your tolerance may be significantly lower than it was before medication.

Very large meals

Even if your appetite occasionally returns, avoid the temptation to eat a large meal. Your stomach is emptying more slowly than usual, and overloading it causes nausea, bloating, and discomfort that can last hours. Three small meals and a snack will always feel better than two large ones.

Carbonated drinks

Fizzy water and soft drinks introduce gas into an already slow-moving digestive system. Many patients find carbonation causes uncomfortable bloating and burping. Still water, herbal teas, and non-carbonated drinks are gentler options.

The eating order that makes a difference

Research on meal sequencing suggests that the order you eat your food affects blood sugar response and satiety. For GLP-1 patients, there's a practical order that maximises nutrition within your limited appetite window:

First: protein. Always. This is the non-negotiable nutrient, and eating it first ensures you get enough even if fullness hits early.

Second: vegetables. Fibre and micronutrients, without many calories. These support digestion and fill nutritional gaps.

Third: complex carbs and fats. The energy-dense components. If you have room, eat these. If you don't, you've already covered the essentials.

This order also tends to produce a more gradual blood sugar response, which helps with sustained energy and reduces the post-meal crashes that some patients experience.

Supplements to consider

When you're eating significantly less food, it's difficult to get all the micronutrients you need from diet alone. Many healthcare providers recommend the following supplements for GLP-1 patients:

A daily multivitamin to cover general micronutrient gaps. This is a reasonable baseline for anyone eating under 1,500 calories daily.

Vitamin D — many people are already deficient, and reduced food intake makes it harder to get from diet. 1,000–2,000 IU daily is a common recommendation.

Vitamin B12 — particularly important if you're taking metformin alongside your GLP-1, as metformin can deplete B12 levels.

Fibre supplement — if constipation is an issue and you're struggling to eat enough vegetables. Psyllium husk is a good option.

Protein powder — not strictly a supplement, but it's the most efficient way to close the protein gap. One scoop added to yoghurt, oats, or a smoothie adds 20–30g of protein with minimal volume.

Talk to your doctor before starting any supplements, especially if you're taking other medications. Blood work can identify specific deficiencies rather than supplementing blindly.

Common mistakes to avoid

Eating too little. Some patients eat under 800 calories a day and assume that's fine because they're not hungry. It's not. Consistently very low intake accelerates muscle loss, causes nutrient deficiencies, and can trigger hair loss, fatigue, and mood problems. If you're regularly eating under 1,000 calories, speak to your doctor about adjusting your dose.

Prioritising carbs over protein. When appetite is low, it's tempting to grab toast or crackers because they're easy. But those easy calories displace the protein your body needs. Make the protein easy instead — pre-cooked chicken strips, Greek yoghurt, boiled eggs, protein shakes.

Skipping meals entirely. One skipped meal can cascade into an entire day of not eating. Try to eat something — even small — at regular intervals rather than waiting for hunger to arrive. On GLP-1s, hunger may never arrive, but your body still needs fuel.

Ignoring hydration. Dehydration mimics the side effects of GLP-1 medications — nausea, headaches, dizziness, fatigue. Many patients attribute these symptoms to the medication when they're actually dehydrated. Track your water intake for a week and see if symptoms improve.

Relying on processed "diet" foods. Low-fat yoghurts, diet bars, and meal replacement shakes often trade fat for sugar and lack adequate protein. Read the labels — if a "diet" product has more sugar than protein, it's not serving your goals.

A sample day

Breakfast: Greek yoghurt with protein powder, berries, and a drizzle of honey (35g protein)

Lunch: Tuna salad over mixed greens with avocado and olive oil dressing (30g protein)

Dinner: Baked chicken breast with roasted broccoli and a small portion of sweet potato (35g protein)

Snack (if hungry): Cottage cheese with cucumber (12g protein)

Total: ~1,300 calories, ~112g protein
