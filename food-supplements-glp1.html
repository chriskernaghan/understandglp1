Essential Supplements on GLP-1: What You Actually Need

Updated Apr 2026·9 min read

The supplement industry has noticed that millions of people are on GLP-1 medications — and it's responded with a flood of products marketed specifically at Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Saxenda users. Some of these are genuinely useful. Many are expensive noise.

The reason certain supplements matter on GLP-1 medications is straightforward: when you're eating 40–60% less food than before, you're also getting 40–60% less of every vitamin, mineral, and micronutrient your body needs. The problem compounds further if nausea is limiting the variety of foods you can tolerate. Over time, specific deficiencies become predictable and worth getting ahead of.

This guide separates what the evidence actually supports from what's being marketed at you. We've grouped supplements into three tiers: essential, worth considering, and skip it.

Before starting any supplement: Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian. Some supplements interact with medications, and dosing needs vary by individual. A blood panel before starting GLP-1 treatment — checking vitamin D, B12, iron, and ferritin at minimum — gives you a useful baseline.

Tier 1: Essential

These are the supplements with the strongest case for most GLP-1 patients. If you're only going to do a few things, do these.

High-quality multivitamin

A good daily multivitamin is the foundation. When you're consistently eating less, a multivitamin provides insurance against the gaps that are hardest to track — the trace minerals, the B vitamins, the fat-soluble vitamins that quietly become depleted over months of reduced intake.

Not all multivitamins are equal. Look for one that provides meaningful doses of B12 (at least 100mcg), folate (as methylfolate rather than folic acid if possible), and zinc. Avoid products with megadose vitamins A or E, which can accumulate to harmful levels. A standard once-daily multivitamin from a reputable brand is sufficient — you don't need anything expensive or GLP-1 branded.

Vitamin D

Vitamin D deficiency is endemic in northern climates regardless of GLP-1 use — the UK government recommends supplementation for everyone between October and March. For GLP-1 patients, the risk compounds because vitamin D is fat-soluble and absorbed alongside dietary fat; when fat intake drops significantly, absorption can suffer.

A standard supplemental dose of 1,000–2,000 IU (25–50mcg) daily is appropriate for most adults. If you're deficient to begin with — which a blood test will confirm — your doctor may recommend a higher loading dose to correct it. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is better absorbed than D2.

Vitamin B12

B12 is worth singling out beyond a general multivitamin because it's one of the most common deficiencies in people eating significantly less, and the consequences of depletion — fatigue, cognitive fog, peripheral nerve damage — develop slowly and are easy to attribute to other causes. B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products, so patients who were already eating less meat, fish, or dairy before GLP-1 treatment are particularly at risk.

If your multivitamin contains a meaningful B12 dose you may be covered. If in doubt, a sublingual B12 supplement (500–1,000mcg, which dissolves under the tongue) bypasses the digestive absorption issues that can reduce uptake from capsules.

Fibre supplement (psyllium husk)

Constipation is one of the most common and most underreported side effects of GLP-1 medications. The drugs slow gastric emptying — useful for appetite suppression, less useful for keeping things moving further down. When food intake drops simultaneously, the problem compounds.

Psyllium husk is the most evidence-backed over-the-counter option. It absorbs water and adds bulk to stool, helping maintain regularity without the cramping that stimulant laxatives can cause. Start with a small dose (around 3–5g in a large glass of water) and increase gradually. Critically: you must drink plenty of water with it — psyllium without adequate hydration can make constipation worse.

Tier 2: Worth considering

These supplements have a reasonable evidence base for specific situations — but they're not universally necessary. Whether you need them depends on your individual circumstances.

Iron (women particularly)

Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency globally, and GLP-1 medications increase the risk for two reasons: reduced intake of iron-rich foods (red meat, legumes, leafy greens), and reduced stomach acid production, which plays a role in non-haeme iron absorption. Women of reproductive age are at highest risk.

Don't supplement iron without a blood test first — iron overload is harmful and supplementing unnecessarily can cause GI side effects that make nausea worse. If your ferritin is low or you have confirmed iron deficiency anaemia, your doctor will likely recommend a specific supplement and dose. Food sources of haeme iron (red meat, organ meats) are better absorbed than supplements, so improving dietary intake where possible is preferable.

Magnesium

Magnesium is involved in hundreds of enzymatic processes, and low intake is associated with fatigue, muscle cramps, poor sleep, and anxiety — all complaints that are common in GLP-1 patients and easy to attribute to the medication itself rather than a nutritional gap. Many people are marginally deficient even before GLP-1 treatment begins.

Magnesium glycinate or magnesium citrate are the best-tolerated forms. Magnesium oxide (the cheapest form, found in many basic supplements) is poorly absorbed and more likely to cause digestive upset. A dose of 200–400mg in the evening is typical. It also has a mild relaxing effect that some patients find helps with the sleep disruption that can accompany early GLP-1 treatment.

Omega-3 (fish oil)

Omega-3 fatty acids are anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular-protective — benefits relevant for the overweight and metabolically unwell patients who are most commonly prescribed GLP-1 medications. When dietary fat intake drops significantly, omega-3 intake from fatty fish, nuts, and seeds also falls.

A standard fish oil supplement providing 1,000mg combined EPA and DHA daily is a reasonable addition. Algae-based omega-3 is available for those avoiding fish products and has equivalent evidence for the key active components.

Biotin

Hair thinning is a well-documented but often unexpected side effect of GLP-1 medications — more accurately, it's a consequence of rapid weight loss rather than the drug itself. The medical term is telogen effluvium: nutritional stress and significant caloric reduction can push hair follicles into a resting phase, leading to shedding that typically starts around months 3–6 of treatment.

Biotin is frequently recommended for hair health, and while the evidence for biotin supplementation in people with adequate levels is modest, it's low-risk and inexpensive. A 5,000mcg daily supplement is commonly used. The more important interventions for hair health on GLP-1 medications are adequate protein intake and overall caloric sufficiency — biotin alone won't reverse thinning caused by protein deficiency.

Calcium (if dairy intake is very low)

Calcium is critical for bone health, and reduced dairy intake on GLP-1 medications can create a gap. Most adults need around 700–1,000mg daily from dietary sources — roughly the equivalent of three portions of dairy per day. If your intake is falling well short of that, a supplement of 500mg calcium carbonate or citrate (calcium citrate is better absorbed and easier on the stomach) is worth considering. Take calcium and iron supplements at different times of day as they compete for absorption.

Tier 3: Skip it

These are supplements frequently marketed at GLP-1 patients that have little meaningful evidence behind them — or where the benefits are better achieved through food and the core supplements above.

"GLP-1 support" supplements

A wave of products marketed as "GLP-1 boosters" or "natural GLP-1 support" have emerged alongside the prescription medication boom. These typically contain berberine, chromium, bitter melon extract, and various other ingredients claimed to naturally raise GLP-1 levels or enhance the effects of medication.

The evidence for most of these ingredients is weak and based on small studies. More importantly, none have been tested for safety or interactions alongside prescription GLP-1 medications. Berberine in particular has real pharmacological activity and can interact with diabetes medications. Approach these products with scepticism — they're capitalising on the GLP-1 brand without the clinical evidence behind it.

Collagen supplements

Collagen is heavily marketed for skin and joint health, and some GLP-1 patients buy it hoping to counter the skin laxity that can come with rapid weight loss. The evidence for oral collagen improving skin elasticity is limited. More importantly, collagen is an incomplete protein — it lacks tryptophan — so it should not be counted toward your daily protein target. The money is better spent on quality whey protein or food-based protein sources.

Digestive enzymes

Digestive enzyme supplements are often recommended anecdotally in GLP-1 communities for nausea and bloating. For most patients, there's no evidence of enzyme deficiency — the nausea and digestive symptoms are caused by slowed gastric emptying, not inadequate enzyme activity, and enzymes won't address that root cause. There's no harm in trying them if you're struggling, but don't expect significant benefit.

Probiotics

Probiotics have genuine evidence in specific contexts — antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, certain IBS presentations — but the evidence for general digestive benefit in GLP-1 patients is weak. The gut microbiome does change with GLP-1 medication use, and this is an active area of research, but we're not yet at a point where specific probiotic strains can be recommended with confidence. Fermented foods (yoghurt, kefir, sauerkraut) provide broader microbiome benefits than capsule probiotics and are a better use of effort and money for most patients.

Practical advice

A few things that make supplementing on GLP-1 medications easier in practice:

  • Take supplements with food where possible. Fat-soluble vitamins (D, A, E, K) need dietary fat to absorb properly. Even a small amount of food helps.
  • Spread them out. Taking everything at once can add to nausea. A multivitamin with breakfast, magnesium in the evening, and fibre at a set time separate from meals works well for many patients.
  • Go slow with fibre. Adding psyllium husk too quickly can cause bloating and cramping. Start with a small dose and increase over two to three weeks.
  • Get bloodwork before and after. Supplementing blind means you don't know what you actually need or whether it's working. A basic nutritional blood panel every 6–12 months is worth it during active weight loss.
  • Tell your doctor what you're taking. This is especially important for iron, magnesium, and anything with pharmacological activity like berberine. Some supplements affect how medications are absorbed.
The supplement stack most GLP-1 patients actually need: A quality multivitamin, vitamin D (especially in winter), B12, and a fibre supplement. That covers the most common gaps without a complicated or expensive regimen. Everything else depends on your individual bloodwork and circumstances.

The bottom line

GLP-1 medications are powerful tools for weight management and metabolic health, but they change the nutritional equation in ways that take real management. The good news is that the supplement list that actually matters is short and inexpensive — a multivitamin, vitamin D, B12, and psyllium husk covers the vast majority of gaps for most patients.

The more important intervention is doing everything possible to maintain food quality and protein intake within the constraints of a suppressed appetite. Supplements are gap-fillers, not replacements for eating well. Prioritise the food first, then use targeted supplements to cover what food can't reliably deliver at reduced intake levels.
