"I Wish Someone Had Warned Me About the Protein Problem"

Feb 12, 2026·10 min read
At a glance: Priya K., 35, lost 32 lbs on Zepbound in four months. The weight was coming off fast and she felt great — until a DEXA scan revealed she'd lost almost as much muscle as fat. Here's how she caught it, what she changed, and the daily protein strategy that turned things around.

The weight was flying off

I started Zepbound in September 2025. My doctor prescribed it for weight management — I didn't have diabetes, but my BMI was 34 and I'd been struggling with my weight since my mid-twenties. I'd tried everything: calorie counting, keto, intermittent fasting. Nothing stuck long-term.

Zepbound worked immediately. The appetite suppression kicked in within the first week. By the end of month one, I'd lost 9 lbs. By month four, 32 lbs. I was ecstatic. My clothes fit differently, my energy was better, my confidence was up. On paper, everything was going perfectly.

Nobody tells you how hard it is to eat enough protein when your appetite is this low. I was barely managing 40 grams a day without realising it.

The DEXA scan wake-up call

A friend suggested I get a DEXA scan — a body composition scan that shows exactly how much of your weight is fat, muscle, and bone. I'd had one done the previous year as a baseline, so I thought it would be satisfying to see the fat loss in black and white.

It wasn't satisfying. It was alarming.

Of the 32 lbs I'd lost, roughly 12 lbs was lean mass. That's muscle. Nearly 40% of my total weight loss was muscle, not fat.

My doctor wasn't surprised when I showed her. She said it's one of the most common issues she sees with GLP-1 patients, especially those who aren't being proactive about protein intake and resistance training. The problem is that when your appetite drops this dramatically, you tend to eat less of everything — and protein is usually the first thing to fall.

Why muscle loss matters: Losing muscle isn't just a cosmetic issue. Muscle is metabolically active tissue — it burns calories at rest, supports your joints, protects your bones, and is one of the strongest predictors of long-term health and mobility as you age. Losing a significant amount of muscle during weight loss can lower your metabolic rate, making it harder to maintain your results and increasing the risk of regaining the weight.

Where I was going wrong

When I looked honestly at what I'd been eating, the problem was obvious. On Zepbound, I simply wasn't hungry. Most days I was eating two small meals and maybe a snack. A typical day looked something like this:

  • Breakfast: Skipped, or a piece of toast with butter
  • Lunch: A small salad with some cheese, or soup
  • Dinner: Whatever my partner was cooking, but a much smaller portion than before
  • Snack: A handful of crackers or some fruit

When I actually tracked my intake for a week, I was averaging about 900–1,000 calories a day and roughly 35–45 grams of protein. For context, the general recommendation for someone trying to preserve muscle during weight loss is around 0.7–1.0 grams of protein per pound of goal body weight per day. For me, that meant I should have been targeting somewhere around 100–120 grams.

I was getting a third of what I needed. And I had no idea.

The appetite problem

Here's the thing nobody really prepares you for: it's not that you're choosing not to eat protein. It's that you're not hungry enough to eat much of anything, and when you do eat, protein-rich foods feel especially heavy and unappealing. A chicken breast when your appetite is this suppressed feels like trying to eat a brick. You gravitate toward lighter, easier foods — crackers, fruit, toast — which are almost all carbohydrates.

I was eating enough to not feel faint. I wasn't eating enough to protect my body composition.

What I changed

My doctor referred me to a dietitian who works specifically with patients on GLP-1 medications. That was a turning point. Here's what we put together:

1. Protein first, every meal

The single biggest change was restructuring every meal around protein. Whatever I eat, the protein goes in first. If I can only manage a small plate, at least the protein gets eaten before I fill up on everything else. This sounds simple but it completely shifted my intake.

2. Protein at breakfast — non-negotiable

I used to skip breakfast entirely or have toast. Now I start every day with at least 25–30 grams of protein. My go-to rotation:

  • Greek yoghurt (the high-protein kind, not the sugary stuff) with a scoop of protein powder mixed in
  • Two eggs scrambled with cottage cheese
  • A protein shake if I really can't face solid food — usually whey protein, milk, and a banana

Getting a strong protein hit first thing made a huge difference. It set the tone for the day and took the pressure off later meals.

3. Strategic snacking

I replaced my carb-heavy snacks with protein-focused ones. Instead of crackers and fruit, I reach for:

  • Beef jerky or biltong
  • Hard-boiled eggs (I batch-prep these on Sundays)
  • Edamame
  • Cheese sticks or babybels
  • Protein bars (I look for ones with at least 20g protein and under 5g sugar)

4. The protein shake safety net

On days where I really can't eat enough — and there are still plenty of those — a protein shake in the evening bridges the gap. It's not ideal, but 30 grams of whey protein in a shake is infinitely better than ending the day at 40 grams total. My dietitian called this the "insurance policy" approach, and it takes the stress out of days when food just isn't happening.

5. Resistance training

The other half of the equation. I'd been doing some walking and the occasional yoga class, but nothing that would actually signal to my body to hold onto muscle. My dietitian was blunt: without resistance training, no amount of protein would fully solve the problem.

I started with two sessions a week at the gym — basic compound movements: squats, deadlifts, rows, presses. Nothing complicated. I was weak at first, which was humbling, but within a few weeks I was getting stronger and it started to feel good rather than like a chore.

My current daily protein targets: I aim for 100–120g of protein per day, spread across meals. A typical split is roughly 30g at breakfast, 30g at lunch, 30g at dinner, and 10–30g from snacks or a shake. Some days I hit it easily; some days I lean on the shake. The key is consistency over perfection.

Three months after the wake-up call

I got a follow-up DEXA scan three months after changing my approach. The results were encouraging:

First 4 months (no strategy)Next 3 months (protein-focused)
Total weight lost32 lbs11 lbs
Fat lost~20 lbs (63%)~10 lbs (91%)
Lean mass lost~12 lbs (37%)~1 lb (9%)
Daily protein35–45g100–120g
Resistance trainingNone2–3x per week

The weight loss slowed — 11 lbs in three months versus 32 in four. But the composition of that loss changed completely. Over 90% of what I lost in the second period was fat. My lean mass barely moved. That's the number that matters.

What I'd tell someone starting Zepbound (or any GLP-1)

  • Track your protein from day one. Don't wait for a DEXA scan to tell you something's wrong. Download a tracking app and log your food for even just the first couple of weeks so you know where you stand. Most people are shocked at how little protein they're actually getting.
  • Get a baseline DEXA scan if you can. It's usually £40–80 (or $50–100 in the US) and gives you real data instead of guesswork. Scales only tell you total weight — they can't tell you whether you're losing fat or muscle.
  • Protein first, always. Make it the foundation of every meal. When your appetite is this low, you can't afford to fill up on carbs and hope there's room left for protein. There won't be.
  • Start resistance training early. You don't need to become a gym rat. Two sessions a week with basic compound lifts is enough to send the signal. Your muscles need a reason to stick around.
  • Don't celebrate rapid weight loss uncritically. I was thrilled about losing 32 lbs in four months. If I'd known nearly 40% of that was muscle, I wouldn't have been celebrating — I'd have been panicking. Fast weight loss without a protein strategy is a red flag, not a win.
  • Ask your doctor about a dietitian referral. This was the single most helpful thing I did. A dietitian who understands GLP-1 medications will give you practical, specific guidance that generic advice can't match.
The protein gap is common: Research suggests that a significant proportion of people on GLP-1 medications aren't meeting minimum protein recommendations. This isn't a willpower issue — it's a direct consequence of the appetite suppression these drugs are designed to produce. Being aware of it is the first step to managing it.

Where I am now

Seven months into Zepbound total. Down 43 lbs from my starting weight. My most recent DEXA shows I've actually gained a small amount of lean mass in the last month — probably from the resistance training catching up. My body composition is shifting in the right direction.

I still have to think about protein every single day. It hasn't become automatic. But I've built enough systems — the breakfast routine, the batch-prepped snacks, the evening shake safety net — that it's manageable rather than exhausting.

The medication is doing its job. I just needed to do mine too.
