GLP-1 and the Brain: Emerging Research on Addiction and Alzheimer's

Updated Feb 2026·14 min read

GLP-1 medications were developed to manage blood sugar and, more recently, to treat obesity. But a growing body of research suggests these drugs do something unexpected in the brain — affecting reward pathways, reducing neuroinflammation, and potentially influencing conditions ranging from alcohol addiction to Alzheimer's disease.

The science is still evolving, and the results so far are a mix of genuine breakthroughs and sobering disappointments. Here's where things stand as of early 2026.

GLP-1 receptors aren't just in your gut

GLP-1 receptors are found throughout the brain, particularly in areas that regulate reward, appetite, and memory. This is why people on Ozempic or Mounjaro often report that "food noise" disappears — the constant mental chatter about what to eat next quiets down. But that same mechanism appears to extend beyond food.

Researchers have found GLP-1 receptors in the mesolimbic dopamine system — the brain's reward circuit. This is the same network involved in addiction to alcohol, nicotine, opioids, and other substances. When GLP-1 medications activate receptors in this system, they appear to dampen the dopamine release that makes addictive substances feel rewarding.

The key insight: GLP-1 medications don't just reduce your appetite for food — they may reduce the brain's appetite for rewarding substances in general. This has opened up an entirely new area of research.

Alcohol and addiction: the most promising frontier

The evidence for GLP-1 medications reducing alcohol consumption is arguably the most exciting development in addiction research in years.

The clinical trial evidence

In February 2025, results from the first randomised controlled trial of semaglutide for alcohol use disorder were published in JAMA Psychiatry. The trial was small — 48 participants — but the results were striking. Even at the two lowest clinical doses (0.25mg and 0.5mg per week, far below the 2.4mg used for weight loss), semaglutide significantly reduced alcohol craving and the amount consumed on drinking days compared to placebo. The researchers described the effect sizes as "promising" — particularly notable given that these were doses much lower than those used for obesity.

The real-world data

Larger observational studies paint an even more compelling picture. A Swedish registry study following over 227,000 people with alcohol use disorder found that semaglutide users had a 36% lower risk of alcohol-related hospitalisation compared to periods when they weren't using the medication. Liraglutide users saw a 28% reduction. These weren't small studies with self-selected participants — this was nationwide registry data with a median follow-up of nearly 9 years.

A separate analysis of real-world data found that semaglutide use was associated with reduced incidence and recurrence of alcohol use disorder, with the protective effect appearing stronger than other GLP-1 medications.

Beyond alcohol

The effects don't appear to be limited to alcohol. Preclinical studies show GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce the intake of various addictive substances in animal models. Registry data has suggested associations with reduced cannabis use disorder. Anecdotally, many patients on GLP-1 medications report reduced interest in smoking, gambling, and compulsive shopping — though these reports haven't yet been confirmed in controlled trials.

Phase 3 trials evaluating semaglutide specifically for alcohol use disorder are now underway, which will provide much more definitive answers about efficacy and optimal dosing.

What patients report: Many GLP-1 users describe a general "quieting" of compulsive urges — not just for food, but for alcohol, smoking, and other habitual behaviours. Scientists believe this reflects the drug's broader effect on the brain's reward circuitry, not just appetite pathways.

Alzheimer's: a promising theory, a disappointing trial

The Alzheimer's story is more complicated — and it's a useful lesson in why preclinical promise doesn't always translate to clinical results.

Why researchers were optimistic

There were good reasons to believe GLP-1 medications might help with Alzheimer's. Multiple observational studies had found that people taking GLP-1 medications for diabetes had significantly lower rates of dementia. One widely cited analysis of electronic health records found that semaglutide users had a 40–70% reduced risk of first-time Alzheimer's diagnosis compared to people taking other diabetes medications. Even compared to other GLP-1 drugs, semaglutide appeared to offer extra protection.

The biological rationale was also strong. Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognised as a driver of Alzheimer's, and GLP-1 medications have potent anti-inflammatory effects. GLP-1 receptors in the brain are found in regions affected by Alzheimer's, and animal studies showed semaglutide could reduce neuroinflammation and improve cognitive function in mouse models of the disease.

The EVOKE trials: what happened

In November 2025, Novo Nordisk announced the results of EVOKE and EVOKE+, two large Phase 3 trials testing oral semaglutide (14mg daily) in nearly 3,800 people with early-stage Alzheimer's. The trials did not meet their primary endpoint — semaglutide showed no significant benefit over placebo in slowing cognitive or functional decline over two years.

The drug did improve several Alzheimer's-related biomarkers, with reductions of up to 10% in markers linked to neuroinflammation. But these biomarker improvements didn't translate into any measurable clinical benefit. Curves for cognitive decline in the semaglutide and placebo groups were essentially indistinguishable.

Based on these results, Novo Nordisk discontinued the planned one-year extension phases of both trials.

What went wrong — and what might still be right

Several factors may explain the disconnect between the observational data and the trial results.

First, there's the question of brain penetration. A substudy found that semaglutide concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid were only about 0.4% of plasma levels — very low. It's unclear whether enough drug was reaching the brain tissue to have an effect.

Second, the observational studies may have been picking up on something different. People with well-managed diabetes who take GLP-1 medications tend to have better cardiovascular health, less inflammation, and better metabolic profiles overall — all of which independently reduce dementia risk. The "protective effect" seen in registry data might reflect these broader health benefits rather than a direct neuroprotective action of the drug.

Third — and perhaps most importantly — there's the prevention vs. treatment question. The observational data showed reduced risk of developing dementia, while the EVOKE trials tested whether semaglutide could slow already-established Alzheimer's. These are fundamentally different questions. As several researchers have noted, GLP-1 medications might still prove valuable for dementia prevention even though they didn't work as a treatment for existing disease.

The bottom line on Alzheimer's: Semaglutide does not treat established Alzheimer's disease. But the question of whether long-term GLP-1 use might help prevent cognitive decline remains open and is still being studied. The observational evidence is suggestive, but we need prevention-focused trials to know for sure.

Other brain-related research

Depression and mood

Several studies have found associations between GLP-1 medication use and reduced depression symptoms, though it's difficult to disentangle the direct neurological effects from the mental health benefits of weight loss itself. Losing significant weight, feeling more energetic, and regaining mobility can all improve mood independently. That said, GLP-1 receptors are present in brain regions involved in mood regulation, and some researchers believe there may be a direct antidepressant effect worth investigating.

Parkinson's disease

A Phase 2 trial of the GLP-1 medication exenatide in Parkinson's disease showed promising results, with improvements in motor function that persisted even after the drug was stopped. This was a small trial, and larger studies are needed. Liraglutide is also being studied for Parkinson's. The rationale is similar to Alzheimer's — neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction play roles in Parkinson's progression, and GLP-1 medications address both.

Traumatic brain injury and stroke

Animal studies suggest GLP-1 receptor agonists may have neuroprotective effects following brain injury and stroke, potentially reducing inflammation and cell death. Human trials are in early stages.

What this means for patients

If you're currently taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss or diabetes, the brain research is interesting context — but it shouldn't change how you think about your treatment. These medications are approved for metabolic conditions, and any neurological benefits are not yet proven enough to factor into prescribing decisions.

A few practical takeaways:

  • If you've noticed reduced alcohol cravings — you're not imagining it. This is a real and increasingly well-documented effect. If you have concerns about your drinking, this could be a good time to discuss it with your doctor.
  • If you're hoping GLP-1s will prevent Alzheimer's — the evidence is not yet strong enough to take these medications for that purpose alone. But maintaining good metabolic health (which GLP-1 medications support) is independently associated with better cognitive outcomes as you age.
  • If you've experienced mood changes — both positive and negative mood effects have been reported. If you notice significant mood changes on a GLP-1 medication, discuss them with your healthcare provider.

What's next

The next 2–3 years will be pivotal for GLP-1 brain research. Phase 3 trials for alcohol use disorder are underway and should provide definitive evidence on whether these medications can be formally repurposed for addiction. Prevention-focused dementia studies, if launched, would take longer but could answer the lingering questions left by the EVOKE trials.

The full EVOKE trial data is expected to be presented at the International Conference on Alzheimer's and Parkinson's Diseases in Copenhagen in March 2026, which may offer additional insights into which patient populations, if any, showed benefit.

What's clear is that GLP-1 medications are far more than simple weight loss drugs. Their effects on the brain — from silencing food noise to dampening alcohol cravings to reducing neuroinflammation — suggest we've only begun to understand what these medications can do.
